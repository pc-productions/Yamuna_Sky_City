Ask people why they want a home by the sea and most will say "the view", and then, after a pause, try to explain something harder to put into words. This article is an attempt at the second part.

## Scarcity, first of all

Every city has a finite coastline, and only a fraction of it can ever hold homes. Ports, industry, public beaches, estuaries and protected land take most of it. What remains is fixed. A sea-facing home cannot be replicated by building another one further inland, and a good site on the water cannot be manufactured later.

This is the simplest reason sea-facing homes hold their appeal across generations of buyers: there will never be many of them. It is also why "sea-facing" is a phrase worth reading carefully. A tower that faces the sea on one side and a road on the other has half its homes facing the road. A building designed so that every residence looks at the water is a different proposition, and a much rarer one.

## Light and air

Anyone who has lived on a coast knows the difference in the quality of light. There is nothing in front of the window but sky and water, so rooms are bright from early morning, and the evening light comes straight in over the sea. The air moves. A home that opens to the water is ventilated in a way an inland apartment, surrounded by other buildings, rarely is.

On the Karnataka coast, this means something specific. The sea breeze in the late afternoon is a daily event; the monsoon, when it arrives, is watched from the balcony rather than merely endured. A [coastal home is a front-row seat](/blog/kulai-and-new-mangalore-understanding-the-areas-growing-connectivity) to weather that most of the country experiences only as an inconvenience.

## The rhythm of the day

A coastline changes the shape of a day in small ways that add up:

- an early walk on the sand instead of a treadmill;
- children who grow up knowing the sea as ordinary rather than as a holiday;
- evenings that have a natural end, when the sun goes down over the water;
- guests who come to visit you, rather than the other way around.

None of this appears on a floor plan. All of it is the reason people who have lived by the sea find it hard to move away from it.

> The view is what you buy. The rhythm is what you keep.

## Height and the horizon

There is a second scarcity beyond the coastline itself: height. From the ground floor, a sea view is a strip between the trees. From the twentieth floor, it is the whole horizon. From the fortieth, it is the curve of the coast in both directions and the shipping lane far out.

Tall coastal buildings are difficult to build well, which is why there are few of them. Wind loads rise with height; salt air is unforgiving to anything not designed for it. That difficulty is part of the value: a tower that has been engineered for the coast offers a view that a low-rise cannot, from homes that are not overlooked by anything.

## Choosing well

If a sea-facing home is on your list, three practical checks are worth making before anything else:

1. **Which homes actually face the water**, and which face the road or the neighbouring plot.
2. **What can be built between the building and the sea** in future. Land use on the coast is regulated, but ask.
3. **How the building has been designed for the coast**: structure, façade, corrosion protection, drainage.
